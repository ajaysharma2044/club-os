# 19 — Economic Capture: Mapping and Monetizing the Money Around College Clubs

*Research date: 2026-09-10. Companion to `07-club-needs-and-funding.md` (funding mechanics), `05-ads-platform.md` (ads), `04-employers.md` (employers). This file quantifies every flow and prices every capture mechanism.*

---

## 0. Executive summary

The founder's thesis — "clubs sit at the center of enormous economic activity; capture as much as possible while staying free" — is **directionally right and structurally harder than it looks.**

Three findings dominate everything else in this report:

1. **The biggest visible pool is the least capturable.** Student activity fees and SGA allocations are $4–8M/year on a 20,000-student campus, but that money never leaves the university's general ledger. It is spent by *reimbursement* against university accounts. Penn State states the rule bluntly: "All RSO's are required to do their 'banking' with the ASA office. **External accounts are prohibited**," explicitly including "personal bank accounts and mobile financial applications" ([PSU ASA](https://studentaffairs.psu.edu/student-life/student-orgs/manage-org/finance-fundraise)). You cannot put a payment rail under money the university already holds.

2. **The capturable pool is the *member-to-club* money the university never touches** — dues, tickets, merch, trip payments, sponsorship invoices, alumni gifts. On a 20,000-student campus that is roughly **$8–12M/year**, today moving through Venmo, GroupMe polls, cash, and personal bank accounts. That is the real prize, and it is unbanked rather than banked elsewhere.

3. **Payments alone will not fund the company.** At realistic capture and realistic take rates, payments yield **$50–150K per large campus per year**. Ads, employer subscriptions, and affiliate revenue are additive and structurally higher-margin. The correct model is Toast's — free software, money on transactions — *stacked with* Nextdoor's — free community, money on local demand. Neither alone clears the bar.

Total realistic annual revenue per 20,000-student campus: **$81K conservative / $275K base / $690K aggressive** — an ARPU of **$4 / $14 / $35**, bracketing Nextdoor's $11.94 and Reddit US's ~$43.

## 1. Quantifying the flows

### 1.1 Student activity fees — the visible pool

Mandatory activity fees are constitutional at public universities provided allocation is viewpoint-neutral (*Board of Regents v. Southworth*, 529 U.S. 217 (2000), 9–0 — [Oyez](https://www.oyez.org/cases/1999/98-1189)). The fee varies by an order of magnitude.

| Institution | Activity fee | Annualized | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Northeastern | $174/semester | $348 | [Huntington News](https://huntnewsnu.com/81863/campus/sga-publishes-club-budget-reports-for-the-first-time-in-years-allocates-4-million-to-student-organizations/) |
| Harvard (HUA) | $450 mandatory (2025, was $200 optional) | $450 | [Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2025/11/10/hua-expects-club-funding-increase-with-required-fee/) |
| Elon | $146/semester ($73 part-time), flat since 2018-19 | $292 | [Elon News Network](https://www.elonnewsnetwork.com/article/2025/08/elon-university-student-activity-fees-fund-various-organizations-campus-events-traditions) |
| UC Berkeley (ASUC) | $33.50/semester undergrad, $27.50 grad | $67 | [LEAD Berkeley](https://lead.berkeley.edu/student-orgs/manage-your-org/fund-your-org/) |
| **Ohio State** | **$37.50–$40/semester** by tuition group | $75–$80 | [OSU Student Activities](https://activities.osu.edu/about/student-activity-fee/) |
| UNH | $89 (FY2019) | $89 | [Wikipedia: Student activity fee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_activity_fee) |
| Penn State | "Student Initiated Fee" (not published as a single figure) | — | [UPAC handbook](https://upac.psu.edu/handbook) |

**Planning range: $70–$450 per student per year; median large public ≈ $150–250.** At 20,000 students that is a **$3–5M annual pool**.

**But only a small slice reaches clubs.** Ohio State publishes the full waterfall and it is the best worked example available: the fee generates **~$4.6M in a typical year**, of which **14.12% goes to student organization funding — roughly $650,000** split across ~1,400 registered orgs, at $500 base operating plus $3,000–$4,500 in competitive program funding ([OSU](https://activities.osu.edu/about/student-activity-fee/)). The other ~86% funds the union, programming board, student media, recreation, and the student government itself. **Assume 15–40%, not 60%, of any fee pool is club-addressable.**

The Florida outliers matter: USF's **$21M** and UCF's **$18.6M** A&SF pools are an order of magnitude above Berkeley's $1.19M, because Florida statute funnels a large mandatory fee through student government. The variance across campuses is roughly **700×** on a per-student basis (UT Austin $0.60 vs Northeastern $183). **Never build a revenue model that assumes a typical SGA pool.**

**Per-org caps are the practical number a treasurer experiences:** Michigan CSG caps any org at **$10,000/semester** across all CSG sources ([CSG policies](https://www.csg.umich.edu/funding-policies)); Ohio State gives **$500 operating + $3,000–$4,500 programming** ([OSU](https://activities.osu.edu/involvement/student-organizations/funding/)); Penn State's UPAC caps food at **$1,000/event**, speakers at **$30,000/yr**, travel at **$5,500/yr**, lodging at **$65/night**, registration at **$100/person**, and funds at most **90% of program cost** ([UPAC](https://upac.psu.edu/handbook)); Berkeley caps new SAGs at **$500 in year 1**.

> **Strategic read:** SGA money is a *retention hook*, not revenue. UT Austin allocating $35,000 across a 52,000-student campus while Northeastern allocates $4M shows how arbitrary this pool is. The product value is a budget-request generator and deadline tracker — it drives usage, not GMV. The Berkeley data shows the median hobby RSO gets **$815/yr**; you cannot build a business on 3% of $815.

NC State chapter housing runs **$4,186–$6,500/semester**, plus internet $150/sem, laundry $50/sem, parlor fees $96–$1,350, and in-house meal plans of $1,200–$2,900 ([fsl.dasa.ncsu.edu](https://fsl.dasa.ncsu.edu/live/costs/)). **Housing and board are 3–6× the dues and are almost entirely uncapturable** — they are billed by the house corporation or the university.

**Three structural facts that shape the product:**

1. **Non-housing dues cluster at $300–$1,000/semester** — much tighter than the "$500–$3,000" folk figure.
2. **NPHC inverts the curve**: $1,000–$3,000 one-time intake, then $100–$300/year ([usm.edu](https://www.usm.edu/fraternity-sorority-life/joining.php), [samford.edu](https://www.samford.edu/greeklife/finances)). A recurring-billing product is the wrong shape for NPHC chapters.
3. **Roughly 40–50% of dues is a pass-through to national HQ.** At San Diego, inter/national dues are **$194–$437 per member per semester** of a $754–$944 total. Alpha Chi Omega's chapter bylaws price the national bundle (per capita + insurance + technology + communication) at **$211–$220/year** ([TCU Iota Lambda bylaws](https://engage.tcu.edu)). *A platform that can remit the national portion automatically is solving a real treasurer problem and touching a second, larger pool of money.*

**Price band: $10–$25 GA, $25–$40 premium/VIP.** Venue capacity is the only available attendance proxy, and it is large: GW's Bhangra Blowout (32nd year, intercollegiate) runs at **DAR Constitution Hall, ~3,700 seats** ([calendar.gwu.edu](https://calendar.gwu.edu/event/bhangra-blowout-xxxii)); Cornell Bhangra's PAO Bhangra XXIII fills **Barton Hall**. Formals run higher, $25–$90.

Two operational details that matter: Rutgers charges a **$1.50 service charge on all online student-org ticket sales** ([sca.rutgers.edu](https://sca.rutgers.edu/campus-involvement/student-organization-handbook)) — proof that a per-ticket fee is already normalized on campus. And a Tufts culture show budgeted **$1,488** total ([Tufts Daily](https://www.tuftsdaily.com/article/2022/04/tcu-senate)), which is the scale most events actually operate at.

Modeling a 20,000-student campus: ~500 registered orgs, ~15% run at least one paid ticketed event, averaging two per year → **~150 events × 250 tickets × $18 ≈ $675K**, plus 8–15 marquee shows (800–2,500 seats at $15–$40) adding **$300–700K**. **Base case $1.0–1.5M in club ticket sales per large campus.**

This is the flow where incumbent economics are worst for the club and best for a platform: Eventbrite charges **3.7% + $1.79 per ticket plus 2.9% per order** ([Eventbrite](https://www.eventbrite.com/organizer/pricing/)). On a $10 ticket that is **~$2.45, or 24%**. Luma charges **5%** flat, waived on a $59/mo Plus plan ([Luma](https://luma.com/pricing)). A club-native product at 5–8% all-in is simultaneously *cheaper for students* and *better margin than payments*.

### 2.2 The Connect account-type decision

- **Standard**: the club has its own full Stripe account; Stripe owns the relationship, KYC, and loss liability; the platform can take an application fee. Lowest risk, worst UX, and the club can leave.
- **Express**: Stripe-hosted onboarding, platform-branded; **the platform bears negative-balance liability**. This is the industry default for marketplaces and the right answer here.
- **Custom / v2 controller properties**: full white-label, full liability, full compliance burden. Premature.

Stripe's Connect docs are explicit that under the direct-charge model "the connected account is responsible for Stripe fees, refunds, and chargebacks" — but in practice, when a club's connected account goes negative after a chargeback and the club has $0 and has graduated, **the platform eats it**. Budget for this (§7).

### 2.3 Issuing, Treasury, and the club debit card

Stripe **Issuing** is $0.10 per virtual card and $3.50 per physical card; the platform shares interchange, which on US credit runs **~1.75% of transaction value to the issuer** (US average interchange ≈ 1.79%, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interchange_fee)). Debit is Durbin-capped and far lower.

Stripe **Treasury** is in **public preview in the US and UK**, offers financial accounts with **no monthly fees or minimum balance**, and holds funds at Fifth Third Bank, N.A. with FDIC pass-through eligibility ([Stripe Treasury docs](https://docs.stripe.com/treasury)). This is the technical substrate for "a real bank account for your club."

**Interchange is the quietly best take rate in the whole map.** If a club runs $12,000/year of spending through a platform-issued card, interchange share at ~0.8–1.2% net is **$96–144/club/year with zero marginal cost and no fee shown to anyone.** Across 200 active clubs on a campus that is $19–29K — comparable to the entire payments line. This is exactly Ramp's and Brex's model, and Ramp proves clubs will accept free software funded this way ([Ramp pricing](https://ramp.com/pricing): $0/month, unlimited physical and virtual cards).

The blocker is that Issuing/Treasury require the platform to know *who the legal entity is* (§7).

### 2.4 Money transmission: why you are probably fine, and exactly why

Two exemptions do the work.

**Federal (FinCEN).** 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii) exempts payment processors. FinCEN's four-part test requires that the entity: (1) facilitates the purchase of goods or services or payment of bills for goods or services, other than money transmission itself; (2) operates through clearance and settlement systems that admit only BSA-regulated financial institutions (ACH, card networks); (3) operates pursuant to a formal agreement; and (4) that agreement is with the seller or creditor that provided the goods or services and receives the funds ([FinCEN administrative ruling](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-regulations/administrative-rulings/application-money-services-business)). Notably, FinCEN declined to grant the exemption in that ruling because disbursements might occur *outside* regulated settlement systems — **so never disburse by any means other than ACH/card rails.**

**State (agent-of-payee).** California Financial Code § 2010(l) exempts "a transaction in which the recipient of the money or other monetary value is an agent of the payee pursuant to a **preexisting written contract** and delivery of the money to the agent satisfies the payor's obligation to the payee" ([Cal. Fin. Code § 2010](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=FIN&sectionNum=2010)). Most states have an analogous provision, harmonized further by the CSBS Money Transmission Modernization Act.

**Practical consequence:** if the club (payee) signs an agreement appointing the platform its agent, and a member's payment to the platform discharges the member's obligation to the club, and funds settle only via card/ACH — the platform is not a money transmitter. **Using Stripe Connect with direct or destination charges keeps you inside this box automatically, because Stripe is the processor of record.** The moment you hold balances for clubs in your own name and let them transfer to each other, you leave it.

### 2.5 Tax

- **1099-K:** the federal threshold is back to **more than $20,000 AND more than 200 transactions** for third-party settlement organizations ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/understanding-your-form-1099-k)), after the 2025 legislation reversed the $600 regime. Most clubs fall below this. But **MA, VT, VA, MD, IL and NJ set their own thresholds as low as $600–$1,000**, so a Massachusetts club collecting $900 of dues generates a 1099-K. **If the connected account is onboarded under a student's SSN, that 1099-K lands on a 20-year-old's tax return.** This is the single most dangerous product detail in the business.
- **Sales tax:** all 50 states plus DC now impose marketplace-facilitator obligations, typically triggered at **$100,000 in sales or 200 transactions** per state ([TaxJar](https://www.taxjar.com/sales-tax/marketplace-facilitator)). If the platform becomes merchant of record for club merch, it becomes the facilitator and must collect and remit — a real but solvable cost (Stripe Tax at 0.5%/txn or $90/mo). Event *admissions* are taxable in a substantial minority of states. **Recommendation: do not become merchant of record for merch in v1.**
- **Entity status:** most clubs have no EIN and are not tax-exempt. Minnesota's guidance is representative: unincorporated groups under ~$5,000 gross receipts "do not necessarily need to file"; above that, file Form 1120 as a taxable organization or pursue 501(c)(3)/(c)(7) ([UMN](https://sua.umn.edu/tax-information)). Most social clubs and Greek chapters would be **501(c)(7), not (c)(3)** — meaning **donations to them are not deductible.** A product that lets a club imply otherwise creates liability.

### 2.6 The Open Collective Foundation failure — study this

OCF was the closest thing to "a fiscal sponsor for small informal groups at scale," and it died.

- Board announced dissolution **February 2024**; fiscal sponsorship ceased **September 30, 2024**; all collectives frozen **October 1, 2024**; dissolution effective **December 31, 2024** ([Open Collective](https://opencollective.com/foundation)).
- Open Collective Inc. put out an emergency call: **"over 600 projects" needing to rehome approximately "$18 million USD"** ([blog](https://blog.opencollective.com/fiscal-sponsors-we-need-you/)).
- Open Collective Inc. stated plainly: "The exact specifics of OCF's decision to dissolve are not known to us," while noting OCF had "increased technological needs" and "has not found a viable and sustainable path forward" ([official statement](https://blog.opencollective.com/open-collective-official-statement-ocf-dissolution/)).
- The timeline reveals the cost drivers: a separate, earlier deadline for **employment** (June 30, 2024, "last day collectives can employ staff") than for spending (September 30). **Payroll and employment law were a distinct, unsustainable burden.**
- The domain `opencollective.foundation` no longer resolves to the organization — it now 301-redirects to an unrelated commercial site. The post-mortem material is effectively gone.

**Lessons, stated bluntly:**
1. Average collective size was **$30,000** ($18M ÷ 600). At a 10% fee that is $3,000/year of revenue against a compliance cost that includes 50-state charitable solicitation registration, Form 990 preparation, employment-law exposure, grant restrictions, and a mailbox full of edge cases. **The unit economics of comprehensive fiscal sponsorship for small groups do not work.**
2. The failure mode is not slow decline; it is a **hard stop with a nine-month wind-down that strands other people's money.** If you are the entity of record for 600 clubs' cash, your insolvency is their catastrophe.
3. Employment is the poison pill. Never let clubs hire through you.

**The counter-example is Hack Club's HCB**, which works: **7% of revenue, no other fees**, including legal entity, bank account, automatic 990 filing, payroll, physical and virtual debit cards (Stripe Issuing, issued by Celtic Bank), and receipt tracking. Scale: **6,500+ projects, $100M+ processed since 2018**; the public stats endpoint suggests ~$124.9M lifetime volume, ~257,000 transactions, ~6,900 organizations, with ~$61M in the trailing year ([HCB](https://hackclub.com/fiscal-sponsorship/), [stats](https://hcb.hackclub.com/stats)). The differences from OCF: a **7% fee that is actually charged on revenue**, a **narrow, homogeneous population** (teen/student technical projects), proprietary software that absorbs the operational load, and a philanthropically subsidized parent. Two of those four are replicable.

| Political campaigns | Episodic; SGA election codes cap spend at $100–$1,500 | **Do not sell.** Offer equal-exposure candidate pages | — |

**Nextdoor is the right analog and the right ceiling.** FY2025 revenue **$257.6M, ARPU $11.94 per WAU, 21.0M WAU, 340K+ neighborhoods, 4.1M claimed business pages, self-serve ≈60% of revenue** ([Nextdoor 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1846069/000184606926000023/kind-20251231.htm)). Q2 2026: **$75M revenue, +15% YoY, 22.9M platform WAU, $10M adjusted EBITDA** ([Nextdoor Q2 2026](https://about.nextdoor.com/press-releases/nextdoor-reports-second-quarter-2026-results)). Nextdoor proves a hyperlocal, low-ad-load, self-serve local ad business can reach ~$12 ARPU and turn EBITDA-positive — and that it takes a decade.

**Retail media is the better strategic frame.** Instacart and DoorDash converted logistics marketplaces into ad businesses because they own the demand signal and the checkout. DoorDash Q2 2026 reported **$33.1B Marketplace GOV, $4.5B revenue, a 13.5% net revenue margin** ([DoorDash Q2 2026](https://ir.doordash.com/news/news-details/2026/DoorDash-Releases-Second-Quarter-2026-Financial-Results/default.aspx)) — and its ad business is layered on top at near-100% incremental margin. **A "campus media network" works the same way only if you own campus checkout.** That is the strongest argument for building payments even when payments revenue alone is thin: *payments produce the purchase data that makes the ad inventory worth 3–5× a generic impression.*

## 6. Strategy: the "free software, money on payments" playbook

### 6.1 What makes it work elsewhere

| Company | Free/cheap software | Monetization | Volume per customer/yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toast | POS below cost | 82% of revenue from fintech | ~$1.3M card volume |
| Square | Free POS, free invoices | 2.6–2.9% processing | $100K–$500K |
| Shopify | $29–$399/mo | Merchant solutions ≫ subscription; ~4% blended take on $292B GMV | $50K+ |
| Wave | Free accounting | Payments + payroll | $50K–$300K |
| Givebutter | 0% platform fee | Donor tips (claims 92% opt-in) | $10K–$500K |
| Zeffy | Literally $0 | Donor tips (~67% opt-in) | $10K–$200K |
| Ramp | Free spend management | Interchange | $500K+ |
| ezCater | Free ordering | Supply-side commission | $20K–$200K |

**The precondition is consistent: high recurring transaction volume per account, concentrated in a single system of record, with an owner who has budget authority.** Clubs fail all three tests individually and pass them only in aggregate.

### 6.2 What must be true for clubs

1. **The club must be able to legally receive money that is not a student's personal money.** This requires an EIN and an account in the org's name. Everything depends on this (§7).
2. **Officer turnover must not break the rail.** A payment link that dies every May is worthless. The product must survive the transition; this is a feature, not a chore.
3. **Volume must aggregate.** 200 clubs × $7,000 = $1.4M — a single mid-size Toast restaurant. The economics only close if a campus is the unit, not a club.
4. **The university must not forbid it.** See §6.4.
5. **Fees must be visible to someone other than the student, or be lower than Venmo's zero.** Venmo is free at the point of use. **Your competitor is not Eventbrite; it is free.** The only durable answers are (a) pass fees to the payer at a rate below Cheddar Up/Eventbrite, (b) charge the supply side, or (c) take interchange.

### 6.3 The counter-model: monetize demand only

Fizz and Handshake represent the alternative: keep everything free for students, charge only advertisers and employers. Evidence for: Fizz reports **4–18% CTR** from nothing more than school-level targeting, and a delivery advertiser converting at **25% vs 1% on Meta**. Evidence against: Handshake built the category, reached 18M students, and its campus-recruiting revenue **plateaued at $150–190M and is softening** — the company pivoted to AI data labeling to reach $1.1B ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/handshake/)).

**Read: demand-side-only has a low ceiling per campus but near-100% gross margin and no regulatory surface. Payments has a higher ceiling, worse margin, and a large regulatory surface — but produces the data that makes the ads worth 3–5×.** Do both, sequenced.

| Risk | Severity | Evidence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Universities ban off-platform payments** | **Critical** | Penn State: "External accounts are prohibited," including "mobile financial applications" | Two product modes: (1) full rail where permitted; (2) ledger + request-generator + reimbursement tracking where not. Sell the second to the university |
| Low volume per club | High | Berkeley median hobby RSO: $815/yr allocation | Aggregate at campus level; lead with Greek, club sports, and competition teams (the $10K+ orgs) |

Student org embezzlement is not hypothetical; it is routine and large relative to the accounts involved.

| Harvard cancer fundraiser (1994) / Hasty Pudding (2002) | $130,000 / $100,000 | — | same |
| UND student-body treasurer (2018) | **$10,000–$50,000** via unauthorized control of a fraternity card | Triggered audit of ~$550,000 in SG funds | [Inforum](https://www.inforum.com/news/und-student-body-treasurer-arrested-on-suspicion-of-embezzlement) |

The lived experience on the other side: a new treasurer "uncomfortable routing funds through personal bank account" is told there is "zero recourse" with payment apps ([r/personalfinance](https://www.reddit.com/r/personalfinance/comments/xxmsxm/treasurer_for_club_at_college_and_confused_about/)); a former treasurer of a dead club is left holding "$250 cash + $300 gift cards" ([r/legaladvice](https://www.reddit.com/r/legaladvice/comments/ljg6nm/treasurer_for_a_university_student_organization/)).

**Required guardrails — these are the product, not overhead:**

1. **Dual approval** above a threshold ($250 is the right default; Michigan already uses $250 as its advance-payment line).
2. **Immutable audit log** with an optional public-transparency view (the HCB/Open Collective pattern — HCB's transparency is the reason advisors trust it).
3. **Per-card and per-committee spending limits**; virtual card per event, auto-expiring.
4. **Receipt-required transactions**, with auto-lock of the card when receipts are overdue (Ramp's mechanic).
5. **Advisor read-only visibility** — this is what converts a university from hostile to neutral.
6. **Officer transition as a first-class flow**: scheduled handoff, re-KYC of the new representative, revocation of the old one, no shared credentials. Account takeover at transition is the highest-probability security event in this product.
7. **Chargeback controls on ticketing**: event tickets are a high-dispute category (the buyer is young, the descriptor is unrecognizable, the event may be cancelled). Set clear statement descriptors (`CLUBNAME*EVENT`), hold payout until 24h post-event for first-time organizers, and budget **$15/dispute** plus loss of the ticket value. Assume 0.4–0.9% dispute rate on student-run events versus ~0.1% e-commerce baseline; that is a real 30–60bp drag on ticketing margin.
8. **KYC for 19-year-old treasurers**: Stripe requires name, DOB, address, SSN last 4 (full SSN or ID document on escalation), and a representative attestation. Stripe Identity at **$1.50/verification** is cheap insurance. But the deeper problem is not verification — it is *whose* identity.

## 10. Deliverable 3 — Legal and entity structure for handling club money

**Recommendation: a three-layer structure, adopted in order. Do not skip layers, and do not attempt Layer 3 before Layer 1 is at scale.**

### Layer 1 (launch): Platform as agent-of-payee; the club is the merchant

- Use **Stripe Connect Express**. Each club gets its own connected account. Stripe is the processor of record and handles KYC; you take an **application fee**.
- The Terms of Service must contain an explicit **agent-of-payee appointment**: the club appoints the platform as its limited agent to receive payments, and a member's payment to the platform **discharges the member's obligation to the club**. This is the precise language of Cal. Fin. Code § 2010(l) and its analogues, and it is what keeps you outside state money transmission licensing.
- Settle **only** through card networks and ACH — never by any other means — to preserve the FinCEN 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii) payment-processor exemption under the four-part test.
- **The single most important product decision: onboard the club as an entity, not as a student.** Build a 10-minute in-product wizard that files **IRS Form SS-4** to obtain a free EIN for the club as an *unincorporated association*, then onboard the connected account as `business_type: company` with the treasurer as `representative`. This:
  - keeps the **1099-K off a 19-year-old's SSN** (critical in MA/VT/VA/MD/IL/NJ where thresholds are $600–$1,000);
  - makes the club, not the student, the legal recipient of funds;
  - unlocks restaurant-fundraiser programs that require a federal tax ID (Chipotle explicitly requires one and will not make checks payable to individuals — [Chipotle FAQ](https://community.chipotle.com/faq/));
  - is the prerequisite for Issuing and Treasury later.
- **Do not custody funds.** Payouts go directly from Stripe to the club's account. You are never the holder of record.
- **Be honest about tax status.** A default club is a taxable unincorporated association or a 501(c)(7) — **not** a (c)(3). Build an entity-status wizard that determines the bucket (under ~$5,000 gross receipts: generally no filing; above: Form 1120 or pursue exemption) and **hard-blocks the club from issuing a tax receipt or using the word "deductible"** unless a real 501(c)(3) is in the chain. Route donors who need receipts to the university foundation's platform — and get credit with the university for doing so.

### Layer 2 (year 2–3): Partner with an existing 501(c)(3) fiscal sponsor for the clubs that need it

- For the minority of orgs that genuinely need deductible donations, grants, or a real bank account, **contract with an existing licensed fiscal sponsor rather than becoming one.** Use a **Colvin Model C (pre-approved grant relationship)** where possible — the project stays legally separate and the sponsor re-grants — because Model C carries far less liability than **Model A (comprehensive)**, in which the project's activities and its people become the sponsor's own.
- Price the partnership so the sponsor's fee is visible and sustainable. **HCB's 7% is the empirical floor that works**; anything at or below 5% for comprehensive sponsorship of small, high-touch groups is how OCF died.
- **Contractual red lines with any sponsor:** no employment or payroll of club members, no assumption of restricted grants, no multi-state charitable solicitation registration obligations flowing to you, and a documented wind-down plan that protects club funds if the sponsor fails. OCF's separate, earlier deadline for ending *employment* (June 2024) versus *spending* (September 2024) is the tell — employment was the unsustainable part.

### Layer 3 (year 4+, only if the data demands it): A separate, independently governed 501(c)(3)

- Charter only if a large cohort of orgs sustains **>$5,000/year each** through the platform and the partner-sponsor economics are provably worse than in-house.
- Structure it as a **legally and financially separate entity** with an independent board, its own reserves (12+ months of operating expense), and a fee no lower than **6–8% of revenue**. The for-profit platform licenses software to it at arm's length.
- **Explicit prohibitions written into the charter:** no employment of project personnel; no acceptance of restricted institutional grants; no international grantmaking; mandatory quarterly solvency reporting to the board; a pre-drafted wind-down plan that transfers projects to named alternate sponsors *before* funds are frozen.
- The failure you are insuring against is precise: **600 projects and $18 million stranded with nine months' notice, and the post-mortem domain expiring within two years.**

### What to tell a university general counsel

> "Club money is already leaving your control — it is in students' personal Venmo accounts, which is where the $200,000 Purdue embezzlement and the $30,000 Harvard case happened. We are not creating a shadow banking system; we are giving each organization its own EIN, its own processor account, dual-approval controls, an immutable audit trail, and read-only advisor visibility. On campuses where external accounts are prohibited, we run in ledger-only mode and hand you better visibility than you have today, for free."

That framing — **risk reduction, not disintermediation** — is the only version of this pitch that survives contact with a Penn State-style policy.

## 11. Conclusions

1. **The addressable money is member-to-club, not university-to-club.** ~$3.5M of routable GMV per 20,000-student campus, against $28–50M of total economic activity touched.
2. **Payments alone yields $50–150K per large campus.** Real, but not a company by itself. Toast's average customer runs 185× a club's volume.
3. **Ticketing is the best first dollar** — the incumbent charges 24% on a $10 ticket.
4. **Food commissions and card interchange are the two best take rates** because neither shows a fee to a student.
5. **Student housing leasing is the largest unserved local ad budget on any campus**, and clubs are the only way to target group-lease intent.
6. **The employer arbitrage is 30×**: Handshake's median contract is $29,835 for a whole school; a club's Gold sponsorship is $1,000 for a pre-qualified pool.
7. **Get the club an EIN.** Everything — 1099-K safety, cards, fundraisers, sponsorship invoicing, fiscal sponsorship — depends on the club being an entity rather than a student.
8. **Stripe Connect Express plus an agent-of-payee clause keeps you out of money transmission.** Never custody, never disburse outside card/ACH rails.
9. **Do not build a fiscal sponsor.** OCF stranded 600 projects and $18M. Partner with one at ≥7%, and never touch employment.
10. **Underwrite at $14 ARPU, not $200.** Base case $275–295K per 20,000-student campus, reachable with a light ad load, a thin payments take, and one employer contract — and defensible against Nextdoor's $11.94.
11. **The fastest path to payment volume is not a campus — it is a national Greek HQ.** $300–600M/year already flows chapter→HQ as a dues pass-through; one HQ contract installs the product at 200–400 chapters that already bank externally and are outside university payment policy (§12).

## 12. Appendix — the national-dues remittance pool (the highest-leverage unmodeled flow)

The single most interesting un-modeled flow surfaced in this research: **40–50% of every Greek member's dues is a pass-through to national headquarters** ($194–$437 per member per semester at San Diego; $211–$220/year for Alpha Chi Omega's national bundle). Chapter treasurers collect this locally and remit it upward, by check or portal, on a schedule, under threat of chapter suspension.

- 750,000 undergraduate members × ~$400–$800/year of national pass-through ≈ **$300–600M/year moving from chapters to HQs.**
- The HQs are large, sophisticated, and already file consolidated group returns (KKG's group return alone: **$79.5M**).
- They have a direct financial interest in chapters collecting dues on time, and in *visibility* into chapter finances — which is exactly what a club OS produces.

**This is a B2B2C distribution channel nobody is using.** Selling a national fraternity or sorority HQ a chapter-finance product — one that automatically remits the national portion, reports arrears, and gives HQ a dashboard — would install the platform at 200–400 chapters in one contract, with the dues volume attached. It is a far faster path to payment volume than campus-by-campus bottom-up adoption, and it sidesteps the university-prohibition problem entirely, because Greek chapters are legally independent of the university and *already* bank externally.

**Recommend adding this as a parallel GTM track in Phase 1.**
