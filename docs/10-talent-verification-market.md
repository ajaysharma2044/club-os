# Club OS — The Talent Verification Market

*Draft v0.1. Covers accelerators, quant trading firms, prestige fellowships, and nonprofit talent pipelines as buyers of a verified student record. This is distinct from the mainstream employer product in `04-employers.md`'s underlying research — this document covers the higher end.*

---

## 1. The number that frames everything

**Handshake reached roughly $1.1 billion in annualized gross revenue by April 2026**, but its legacy campus recruiting business — selling access to unverified, self-reported student profiles — is only $150 to $200 million of that, at about 80% margin, across 20 million students and 500,000-plus employers.

The rest came from pivoting into AI training data, going from zero to $80 million in eight months. **Handshake's own pivot is the tell: verified capability monetizes far better than a job board.**

Every buyer in this document has already demonstrated dissatisfaction with unverified self-reported data, by building their own competitions, endorsement committees, or reference-check operations to replace it. **That gap between what verification costs today and what unverified data sells for is the opportunity.**

## 2. Quant trading firms: budget is not the constraint, differentiated signal is

Compensation at the top is now extraordinary and rising.

|---|---|---|---|
| Optiver | $30,333 | $10K | $300–420K |
| D.E. Shaw | $30,001 | $20K + housing | $280–390K |
| Citadel / Citadel Securities | $25,133 | $5–25K + $15K housing | $400–700K |
| Jane Street | $25,000 | $40K | $387K |
| Hudson River Trading | $25,133 | $25K | $467–495K |
| SIG | $26,000 | $10–27.5K | $148K (associate) |

**Citadel is the only firm that publishes selectivity: 350 interns from 115,900-plus applications for its 2026 class, a 0.36% acceptance rate**, drawn from 90 universities, 90% STEM, including IOI, IMO, and USACO Platinum medalists.

**Jane Street's entire intern program costs roughly 0.03% of its comp pool.** These firms are not budget-constrained. They are constrained by finding signal competitors cannot also see, which is why they build proprietary assessments rather than buy commodity data.

### The pattern that matters most: mass algorithmic screening at near-zero cost per candidate

**IMC's Prosperity trading competition drew 18,803 teams across 500-plus universities on a $50,000 total prize pool.** That is roughly $2.66 per team, against a documented campus cost-per-hire of $6,275. **IMC's entire annual competition budget costs less than eight ordinary campus hires.**

Jane Street runs the same play with a five-week residential program for incoming first-year students, paying tuition, housing, and a $5,000 scholarship per participant, explicitly selecting on **"technical accomplishments including product user base, published research, and open-source contributions"** rather than any credential. That is, verbatim, the specification for a verified-achievement dataset.

Two things follow. First, **premier competitive math draws firms no dollars at all**: the Putnam competition runs on a 1927 endowment, drawing 3,988 students from 477 institutions in 2024, and firms simply watch the public results. Second, **firms build private competitions specifically because the public ones are free and observed by every competitor.** A verified record of who actually organized, led, and shipped inside their clubs is exactly the kind of proprietary signal a Jane Street or a Citadel would pay to see before their competitors do.

## 3. Prestige fellowships: an existing, unpriced verification industry

This is the part of the market that already runs on manual verification, at real institutional cost, and it maps almost exactly onto what our record produces.

| Program | Applicants | Winners | Institutional cap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rhodes (US) | ~2,500 | 32 | Institutional endorsement required |
| Marshall | ~1,000 | 40–50 | **Max 24 endorsed applications per institution** |
| Truman | ~800 | 55–65 | **Max 4 nominees + 3 transfer nominees per school** |
| Churchill | n/d | up to 18 | **150-plus institutions, max 2 nominees each** |

**Every one of those caps forces a university to run an internal competition, and that internal competition is an unpriced, labor-intensive act of achievement verification, performed thousands of times a year across the country.**

Marshall's process is the clearest statement of what is actually being bought: a single designated advisor "verifies applications are true, original and in accordance with the Candidate Rules," then submits an endorsement letter from the president, provost, or dean explaining how the candidate performed during institutional selection.

**The stated criteria are almost all leadership, not grades.** Truman: "the record of campus and community service ... are the most important criteria." Gates Cambridge names "capacity for leadership" as one of four criteria. Every one of these is precisely what a verified club record contains: positions held, budgets managed, teams led, events run.

**Institutional spend on this verification is estimated at $60 to $100 million a year across roughly 500 to 700 US institutions**, each running a fellowship-advising office. That spend buys manual verification and narrative construction for roughly 15,000 applications chasing 2,000 awards.

## 4. The two organizations that already sell exactly this

Two nonprofits already operate a business model of selling employers vetted, verified student talent, and their financials are the best available price signal in the entire market.

**INROADS** is the cleanest comparable. Roughly $3.7 million of its program revenue comes from corporate placement fees, against historically 800 to 2,000 interns placed a year. **That implies a fee of roughly $1,850 to $4,600 per intern placed** — the single most defensible per-student price point found anywhere in this research. INROADS is also losing money, which is a caution about the underlying cost structure, not the price.

**Management Leadership for Tomorrow** runs 225 employer partners who explicitly pay "in exchange for ... access to fellows," supporting 7,800-plus fellows a year, with 2,000-plus hired annually by partner employers. Program-service revenue of $4.8 million across 225 partners implies **roughly $21,000 per partner per year** as an order-of-magnitude floor.

**ColorStack runs a tiered corporate partnership model where Jane Street is a Gold partner and Two Sigma and Meta are Copper partners.** This is direct evidence that quant and technology firms already pay nonprofits for curated access to verified high-achieving students. Pricing is not public, but the willingness to pay is confirmed at the very top of the market.

## 4b. A correction: venture scouts, checked directly, are a much smaller line than framed earlier

A dedicated pass on VC scout programs specifically (not accelerators, not quant firms) came back with a real but sobered verdict, and it corrects something said earlier in this conversation.

**The realistic buyer pool is a few hundred firms, not a market.** Estimates of actively investing US VC firms range from 585 that closed a fund in 2025 to a few thousand in broader databases, but the number that both invest early and specifically care about campus-stage talent sourcing, the Dorm Room Fund and Contrary Capital archetype, is realistically **low hundreds of firms**.

**Two firms are already paying for exactly this job, manually.** Contrary Capital staffs more than 50 Venture Partners embedded at named universities including Berkeley, MIT, and Stanford, functioning as human talent scouts. Neo runs an entire fund thesis around finding the best computer science undergraduates before anyone else does. **A verified activity graph is a direct productivity multiplier on a budget line they already spend**, which is the strongest argument for them as buyers.

**But two of the highest-profile programs people assume are relevant turned out, on inspection, to be a poor fit.** Sequoia Arc and a16z Speedrun both select already-operating companies and founders, not students by club activity. Their bar is a shipped product, not a club title, which shrinks the addressable list further than it first appears.

**Realistic pricing: $5,000 to $25,000 a year per firm**, well below what a full platform like PitchBook or Harmonic.ai commands, because this is a narrow feed rather than a market-intelligence platform, and the buyer already has a cheaper manual alternative in place. At full penetration across a realistic buyer pool, this is a **low-to-mid single-digit-million-dollar total market**, and first-year penetration of a new, unproven data product sold to a conservative, relationship-driven buyer would likely be well under 20%.

**The honest verdict, stated plainly: this is a real, logical adjacency, not a fantasy, and it is not a second core business.** It belongs exactly where section 6 already placed it: a small number of named-account pilots, Contrary and Neo first, pursued opportunistically after the employer product has real volume. It should never appear as a headline number in a fundraising narrative.

## 6. What this means for the product

**This is a real adjacent line, not the core business, and it should be positioned that way.** The employer product in `04-employers.md` remains the primary revenue engine. This is the premium tier sitting on top of the same verified record, sold to buyers who already pay far more per person than a mainstream employer does.

**The product design is identical to the employer product**, with one addition: these buyers want **early access to rising signal**, not a static database. Antler and quant firms both operate on urgency, wanting to know who is about to become exceptional before a competitor does. That argues for an alert-style product on top of the same evidence base, not a separate database.

**The ethical design does not change.** Same rule as employers: evidence, never a score. Per-audience, revocable consent. No ranking that could function as an automated decision tool. A quant firm or a venture scout gets to see verified facts, exactly as an employer does, and makes its own judgment.

**Sequencing.** This tier comes after the employer product has real volume, not before. It is a premium SKU layered on an already-proven graph, sold to a small number of very well-funded, very motivated buyers, priced closer to $50,000 than $5,000, because that is what the comparables already support.
